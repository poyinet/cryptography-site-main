# Enigma Cryptanalysis

## principle

**Attack**: Enigma Cryptanalysis (恩尼格玛破译). The Computer History Museum timeline describes the British Bombe: "Built as an electro-mechanical means of decrypting Nazi ENIGMA-based military communications during World War II, the British Bombe is conceived of by computer pioneer Alan Turing and Harold Keen of the British Tabulating Machine Company. Hundreds of allied bombes were built in order to determine the daily rotor start positions of Enigma cipher machines, which in turn allowed the Allies to decrypt German messages." The target of the operation was recovering the daily rotor start positions.

## why

The same timeline entry records the lineage of the method: “The basic idea for bombes came from Polish code-breaker Marian Rejewski's 1938 "Bomba."” — the British device extended earlier Polish work. The Bletchley Park Trust site's Enigma summary describes the machine itself: "The Enigma machine was invented by a German engineer Arthur Scherbius shortly after WW1. The machine (of which a number of varying types were produced) …", noting multiple variants that had to be handled.

## used

Both sources stress scale. The Bletchley Park page states that "Nearly 10,000 people worked in the wider Bletchley Park organisation", and its Bombe summary says "These Alan Turing-designed devices changed the course of the Second World War, saving millions of lives in the process". The CHM entry gives the machine count and the effect: "Hundreds of allied bombes were built … which in turn allowed the Allies to decrypt German messages".

## broken

The limits of this route are visible in the wording: the Bombe worked by searching "daily rotor start positions", that is, determining the rotor start positions day by day. The Bletchley Park page is also a public visitor summary — the saved content is the site's summary layout, with the full narrative absent — so this entry uses only its checkable sentences and does not treat it as a substitute for a technical account.
