SM4 Block Cipher

Principles

Algorithm: SM4 (SM4 中国商用分组密码). ### 128-bit block, 32 rounds

SM4 has a 128-bit block and a 128-bit key, uses 32 rounds, and updates one word per round with an 8-bit S-box and a linear transform. The plaintext is split into four 32-bit words X0, X1, X2, X3; each round computes one new word:

X_{i+4} = X_i ⊕ T(X_{i+1} ⊕ X_{i+2} ⊕ X_{i+3} ⊕ rk_i). T applies an 8-bit S-box to each byte and then a linear transform L(B) = B ⊕ (B<<<2) ⊕ (B<<<10) ⊕ (B<<<18) ⊕ (B<<<24). After 32 rounds, a reverse transformation produces the ciphertext. ### Key schedule and decryption

The key schedule also runs 32 rounds, expanding the master key MK through system parameters FK and fixed constants CK into 32 round keys; it uses L’(B) = B ⊕ (B<<<13) ⊕ (B<<<23). Decryption uses the same structure with the round keys in reverse order. ### Engineering consequences

Updating one word per round lets encryption and decryption share a datapath and keeps hardware small. The S-box supplies nonlinearity, while shifts and XOR supply diffusion. This is not AES’s SPN structure, so AES instructions and lookup-table optimizations do not transfer directly.

Why it matters

SM4 is the symmetric base of China’s commercial cryptography system. WAPI wireless LAN, national VPNs, the TLCP record layer, cryptographic modules and financial terminals all need one common 128-bit block cipher; SM4 supplies that primitive and, with SM2 and SM3, completes the suite.

Its engineering value is low implementation cost: an 8-bit S-box plus 32-bit addition and shifts is enough for smart cards, embedded devices and hardware security modules. It is not “a stronger AES”; it is the symmetric entry point of a different standards ecosystem.

Applications

SM4 must be used with a mode. Common combinations are SM4-CBC with HMAC-SM3, SM4-GCM and SM4-CTR. TLCP uses ECDHE_SM4_CBC_SM3 and ECDHE_SM4_GCM_SM3 suites.

Requirements: a 128-bit key, a unique IV or nonce, authenticated-then-decrypt or EtM for CBC, and no GCM nonce reuse. Do not use raw ECB on structured data, and do not reuse the same key material for SM4 and SM3.

Limits & attacks

No practical key-recovery attack on full SM4 is publicly known, but reduced-round differential and linear cryptanalysis, side channels and related-key analysis remain real concerns. CBC padding oracles, GCM nonce reuse and non-constant-time MAC checks break deployments directly.

Implementation boundaries: use certified modules and a real random source; protect S-box tables against cache timing; compare padding and tags in constant time; cap messages per GCM key; prefer forward-secret ECDHE suites in TLCP and disable downgrade fallbacks.

History

SM4’s predecessor SMS4 was designed by Shu-Wang Lu and others, published in 2003 as part of the WAPI national standard GB 15629.11-2003, published independently by OSCCA in 2006, and renamed SM4 in GM/T 0002-2012 in 2012. GB/T 32907-2016 was published in August 2016 and took effect in March 2017; ISO/IEC 18033-3 Amendment 1 added SM4 in June 2021.

Two confusions persist: SMS4 is the old name, and SM4 is both the algorithm and the standard. It was first designed for wireless LAN security and only later became a general commercial block cipher.
