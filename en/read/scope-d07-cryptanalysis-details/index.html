Principles

Domain: Cryptanalysis.

Cryptanalysis is not guessing a key; it exploits structure, statistical bias, implementation leakage or mathematical weakness to reduce the search space to a computable range.

This page settles the 12 subtopics in this domain that do not yet have their own article:

  • cold-boot (Cold Boot): A cold-boot attack exploits key material that survives briefly in memory after power-off; it shows that keys in RAM are part of the attack surface.
  • Algebraic Cryptanalysis (代数密码分析): Algebraic cryptanalysis writes a cipher system as low-degree polynomial equations and uses linearisation, XL or SAT solvers to recover the key.
  • Generic Composition and Search Attacks (通用组合与搜索): Generic composition studies how encryption and a MAC combine into an authenticated-encryption scheme, and which combinations are provably secure in which model.
  • Integral and Higher-Order Differential Attacks (积分与高阶差分): Integral attacks track bit balance through multi-round integral distinguishers, often combined with higher-order differential and zero-correlation linear distinguishers.
  • Linear Cryptanalysis (线性密码分析): Linear cryptanalysis finds linear approximations among plaintext, ciphertext and key bits, turning key recovery into a statistical decision by accumulating bias.
  • Quantum Attacks (量子攻击): Quantum attacks split into Shor-type algorithms that break public-key hard problems and Grover-type algorithms that give a square-root speed-up for symmetric search; their protocol impact differs.
  • Side-Channel Attacks (侧信道): Side channels exploit timing, power, electromagnetic, cache or fault leakage to bypass mathematical proofs; evaluation must include the implementation environment.
  • Steganalysis (隐写分析): Steganalysis decides whether a carrier hides a secret, using statistical tests, feature classifiers and carrier-model comparison; detection and robustness trade off.
  • grover-s-algorithm (Grover's Algorithm): Grover's algorithm gives a square-root speed-up for unstructured search; against a 128-bit symmetric key it reduces brute force from 2^128 to about 2^64.
  • known-plaintext (Known Plaintext): A known-plaintext attack assumes the adversary holds some plaintext and its ciphertext to recover key structure or build a codebook; it is especially common in classical and codebook eras.
  • meet-in-the-middle-attack (Meet-in-the-Middle Attack): A meet-in-the-middle attack uses a time-memory trade-off to reduce key search from 2^n to about 2^(n/2), often against double encryption and intermediate block states.
  • speculative-execution-side-channel (Speculative Execution Side-Channel): Speculative-execution side channels use cache traces left by out-of-order or predicted execution to read data that should not be accessible; Spectre and Meltdown are canonical examples.

Why it matters

These subtopics are kept on one page so that the domain boundary and a minimal definition exist before deciding which ones need independent deep entries. It avoids empty placeholders and avoids mistaking “has a name” for “has been explained”.

Key disputes

  1. Definition boundary: a domain label is a finding aid, not one theory. Merging adjacent entries hides differences in assumptions, models and adversary capability.
  2. Security boundary: catalog registration is not implementation security; standard clauses, parameters and side-channel exposure must be checked separately.
  3. Historical narrative: commemorative accounts of people, institutions and events do not replace primary archives; dates and priority still need item-by-item checking.

Limits & attacks

Boundary: this is a domain-level subtopic settlement page, not a full paper for each subtopic. A one-line definition states only what it belongs to and what problem it addresses; it does not replace a standard, textbook or paper. If an independent entry is created later, it must earn its own bilingual body and evidence review.

Do not mix in

  • Do not treat the domain page as a complete paper for every entry.
  • Do not treat the number of catalog registrations as the number of verified conclusions.
  • Do not treat commemorative narrative in historical entries as technical proof for the domain.
