TLCP Transport Layer Cryptography Protocol

Principles

Protocol: TLCP (TLCP 国密 TLS). ### Record layer and handshake

TLCP defines a record layer, a handshake protocol family and key calculation. The record layer fragments, MACs and encrypts upper-layer data; the handshake negotiates the version, cipher suite, certificates and keys. It follows TLS’s layered design, but its cipher suites and certificate system are national. ### National cipher suites

The standard lists ECDHE_SM4_CBC_SM3, ECDHE_SM4_GCM_SM3, ECC_SM4_CBC_SM3 and ECC_SM4_GCM_SM3, plus IBSDH, IBC and RSA variants. Key exchange uses SM2 in ECC/ECDHE modes (or RSA/IBC); the record layer uses SM4-CBC or SM4-GCM; integrity and the PRF use SM3. ### Dual certificates

TLCP separates a signing certificate from an encryption certificate: the signing certificate proves identity, while the encryption certificate participates in key exchange and encryption. This dual-certificate system is the most visible difference from ordinary single-certificate TLS deployments.

Why it matters

TLCP solves the problem of bringing national algorithms into the TLS ecosystem. It combines SM2, SM3 and SM4 into an interoperable transport protocol, so browsers, VPN gateways, government systems and financial terminals can negotiate national cipher suites inside one framework.

Without TLCP, SM2, SM3 and SM4 would remain separate algorithms. With it, certificates, handshakes, key derivation, record protection and alerts form a complete chain.

Applications

It is used in SSL VPN gateways, national-cipher browsers, government clouds, financial networks and trusted-computing systems. Deployments usually need a dual-certificate CA, SM2 certificate chains, SM3 signatures and SM4-GCM/CBC cryptographic modules.

Read this entry carefully: TLCP is not “TLS 1.3 with SM algorithms swapped in”. Its handshake and cipher-suite definitions are its own, and ordinary TLS endpoints usually cannot interoperate without both sides supporting TLCP or a protocol gateway.

Limits & attacks

TLCP’s security boundary is not the algorithm alone but protocol configuration and implementation: CBC suites need constant-time MAC and padding checks, GCM suites need nonce uniqueness, dual-certificate chains must bind signing and encryption certificates correctly, and downgrade, version rollback, certificate substitution and side channels must be handled.

It is not “secure by default”: changing algorithms while keeping weak randomness, broken certificate validation or old-version fallback still leaves the link vulnerable.

History

TLCP’s predecessor was GM/T 0024-2014, the SSL VPN specification published in February 2014. It built on a TLS 1.1 framework, added ECC/IBC authentication and key exchange, and redefined national cipher suites. In April 2020, GB/T 38636-2020, Transport Layer Cryptography Protocol (TLCP), was published and took effect in November 2020, turning the industry standard into a national standard.

The 2023 revision of GM/T 0024 added Hello extensions; TLCP and TLS 1.3 remain different protocols.
