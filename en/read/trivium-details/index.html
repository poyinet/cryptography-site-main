Trivium Stream Cipher

Article review and catalog-metadata acceptance are recorded separately. The library remains under construction.

Principles

Algorithm: Trivium (Trivium 流密码). ### A 288-bit state in three NLFSRs

Trivium has a 288-bit state split into three nonlinear feedback shift registers A, B and C of lengths 93, 84 and 111. The 80-bit key is loaded into the first 80 bits of A, the 80-bit IV into the first 80 bits of B, the last three bits of C are set to 1, and the remaining bits are zero. ### Three bits updated per clock

Each clock computes

t1 = s66 ⊕ s93 ⊕ s91·s92 ⊕ s171

t2 = s162 ⊕ s177 ⊕ s175·s176 ⊕ s264

t3 = s243 ⊕ s288 ⊕ s286·s287 ⊕ s69

The three new bits shift into the heads of A, B and C while the other bits shift right; the output bit is z = t1 ⊕ t2 ⊕ t3. The registers are connected end to end, so each register feeds another. ### 1152 initialization clocks

After loading, the cipher runs 4×288 = 1152 blank clocks and discards the output, diffusing the key and IV through all 288 bits. Only then does it produce keystream, up to 2^64 bits for one key/IV pair.

Why it matters

Trivium targets minimal hardware area: 288 flip-flops, a few AND and XOR gates, no S-box, lookup table or large memory. It is an eSTREAM hardware-portfolio member and an ISO/IEC 29192-3 lightweight stream-cipher standard, suitable for RFID, sensors and gate-count-limited embedded chips.

Software can use bitslicing or 64-way parallelism for respectable throughput, but hardware is its main home. Compared with AES-CTR, Trivium is far smaller and lower-power, at the cost of one output bit per clock and a long initialization.

Applications

Trivium serves lightweight IoT links, RFID and sensor hardware crypto cores, and data-stream encryption on constrained devices; ISO/IEC 29192-3 standardizes its keystream generator. It provides confidentiality only, so a MAC or AEAD layer is required for integrity.

On the software side it is a standard cryptanalysis benchmark: cube attacks, guess-and-determine attacks and algebraic side-channel analysis all use Trivium because its structure is simple, its rounds are adjustable and its state can be fully modeled.

Limits & attacks

Trivium security depends on the full 1152-clock initialization. Published attacks mostly target reduced rounds, such as cube attacks on 784 or 799 rounds and guess-and-determine attacks on reduced initialization; no practical key recovery is public for the full 1152 rounds, but reduced-round analysis is not permission to cut initialization.

A synchronous stream cipher requires a unique IV; reusing the same key/IV directly exposes plaintext XOR. Trivium has no built-in authentication, and the 2^64-bit keystream limit is a hard boundary.

Physical implementations also need side-channel defense: IET 2024 algebraic side-channel analysis recovers the full 288-bit state from a single power trace on 8/16/32-bit microcontrollers in a short time; ASIC implementations were not broken in that leakage model, but hardware is not automatically safe. Masking, dual-rail logic and power balancing remain necessary.

History

De Cannière and Preneel designed Trivium in 2005, submitted it to eSTREAM and reached the hardware portfolio; it became a member of the final eSTREAM portfolio in 2008. ISO/IEC 29192-3 standardized it alongside Enocoro in 2012. It has since become a standard object of study for lightweight stream ciphers and cube attacks.
