SIV Deterministic Authenticated Encryption

Article review and catalog-metadata acceptance are recorded separately. The library remains under construction.

Principles

Algorithm: Synthetic IV (SIV 确定性认证加密). ### Synthetic IV and two keys

SIV splits the key K into two halves: K1 drives a PRF/CMAC and K2 drives CTR. It first computes S2V over associated data AD1…ADn and plaintext P to obtain a synthetic IV V, then encrypts P with CTR using V as the initial counter. The output is V || C; V is both the counter start and the authentication tag. ### S2V is not plain CMAC

S2V compresses multiple variable-length inputs into one block: it iterates dbl and XOR over the first n−1 inputs; the last input is xorend if at least 128 bits, otherwise doubled, padded and XORed; a final CMAC produces V. This handles multiple AD components without a separate length encoding. ### Deterministic vs nonce-based

Without a nonce, SIV is deterministic: the same key, plaintext and AD produce the same ciphertext, which suits key wrapping. Putting a random nonce into AD yields a nonce-based, misuse-resistant AEAD that retains DAE security under nonce reuse.

Why it matters

SIV solves the problem of encrypting safely when no reliable nonce exists. Key wrapping, disk blocks and database fields often lack a unique nonce; ordinary GCM/CBC fails catastrophically on nonce reuse. SIV derives the IV from plaintext and AD, so nonce reuse at most reveals that identical plaintext/AD produced identical ciphertext; it does not leak the CTR keystream.

It is also the standard construction for deterministic authenticated encryption (DAE): same input, same output; decryption verifies the tag before releasing plaintext, which makes it natural for key wrapping.

Applications

SIV is used for key wrapping (AES-SIV commonly uses 256/384/512-bit keys), DNS records and JOSE A128SIV; with a nonce it serves as a misuse-resistant AEAD.

Requirements: derive K1 and K2 independently; fix S2V input order and AD domain separation; do not encrypt low-entropy guessable plaintext deterministically without randomization; SIV itself outputs a 128-bit synthetic IV and a fixed tag length.

Limits & attacks

SIV security rests on CMAC being a PRF, CTR being IV-based encryption and correct key separation. Nonce reuse does not leak the keystream as in GCM, but deterministic mode reveals that two inputs are identical; low-entropy plaintext can be enumerated and verified.

Implementation boundaries: implement doubling and padding correctly; verify V before decrypting or releasing plaintext; do not treat V as an ordinary public IV and skip verification; key wrapping must resist downgrade and key-separation errors.

History

SIV was proposed by Rogaway and Shrimpton at EUROCRYPT 2006 as a provably secure treatment of key wrapping; Harkins specified AES-SIV in RFC 5297 in 2008. It is not AES-GCM-SIV: RFC 8452 defines a different nonce-based construction.

SIV later appeared in JOSE A128SIV/A256SIV algorithms, but never became a mainstream TLS suite.
