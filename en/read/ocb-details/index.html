OCB Authenticated Encryption Mode

Article review and catalog-metadata acceptance are recorded separately. The library remains under construction.

Principles

Algorithm: Offset Codebook (OCB 偏移密码块模式). ### Offsets and one pass

OCB3 derives a sequence of offsets Δ[i] from nonce N. Each plaintext block is XORed with an offset, encrypted by the block cipher and becomes a ciphertext block; the plaintext block is also accumulated into a checksum. The final block uses a special offset and length handling, and the tag is the encrypted checksum XORed with an offset. ### Associated data

AD is authenticated but not encrypted: an offset-based MAC compresses it into a value that is folded into the tag. OCB needs only the encryption direction of the block cipher, all blocks can be parallelized, and one key with one pass adds only the tag length to the ciphertext. ### Parameters

RFC 7253 requires a 128-bit block, allows nonces up to 120 bits (96 bits recommended), and supports configurable tag lengths; AES-OCB commonly uses a 128-bit tag, while TLS drafts use 96 bits. A nonce must be unique under one key.

Why it matters

OCB’s selling point is one pass, one key and parallelism: authentication is woven into encryption, avoiding the two passes and two keys of Encrypt-then-MAC. On CPUs with AES instructions, OCB throughput approaches CTR, which suits low-latency networks and embedded devices.

It is also a key name in AEAD history: OCB1 in 2001, OCB3 in 2011, RFC 7253 in 2014. OCB2 was completely broken in 2019, which shows why the version must be stated explicitly and OCB2 must never be used as OCB3.

Applications

OCB is used in low-latency networking, disk/file encryption, embedded protocols and some TLS 1.2 drafts; OCB1 was an optional WRAP in IEEE 802.11i, OCB2 entered ISO/IEC 19772:2009, and OCB3 is RFC 7253.

Requirements: unique nonces; at most 2^48 blocks under one key; tags of at least 96/128 bits; never treat OCB2 as OCB3; verify the tag before releasing plaintext.

Limits & attacks

OCB is a nonce-respecting AEAD: encrypting two different messages with the same key and nonce breaks both confidentiality and authenticity, and an attacker can recover authentication secrets and forge later messages. RFC 7253 explicitly points such settings to SIV.

Implementation boundaries: partition nonce counters; bind tag length; cap key usage at 2^48 blocks; verify before decrypting; the 2019 OCB2 attack shows why the version and implementation must be locked to OCB3.

History

OCB1 was proposed by Rogaway, Bellare, Black and Krovetz in 2001; OCB2 followed in 2004; OCB3 was proposed by Krovetz and Rogaway in 2011; RFC 7253 appeared in 2014. OCB2 entered ISO/IEC 19772:2009 but was shown forgeable and decryptable by Inoue–Iwata–Minematsu–Poettering in 2019.

Patents: Rogaway held four US patents, then intentionally let them lapse and placed his OCB-related IP in the public domain. Whether other AE patents apply is still hard to determine, so commercial deployments should perform an IP review.
