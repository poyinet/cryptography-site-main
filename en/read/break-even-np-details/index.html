NP-Completeness of PARTITION

Principles

Theorem: NP-Completeness of PARTITION (平分问题（PARTITION）NP 完全性). ### Decision problem

Input a multiset S={a_1,…,a_r} with total ΣS=2B; decide whether a subset T⊆S has ΣT=B. The certificate is T: add its elements and compare with B in O(r) time, so PARTITION ∈ NP. ### Karp reduction

Karp 1972 reduces from KNAPSACK. Given (a_1,…,a_r,b), construct

c_i=a_i (i=1,…,r), c_{r+1}=b+1, c_{r+2}=Σa_i+1−b. The new total is Σc=2(Σa_i+1), so set B′=Σa_i+1. If the original KNAPSACK has a subset summing to b, take the corresponding elements plus c_{r+1} to reach B′; conversely, any subset summing to B′ either contains c_{r+1} and leaves b, or contains c_{r+2} and leaves b. ### Conclusion

Thus KNAPSACK ≤_p PARTITION. Karp's Main Theorem lists PARTITION among the NP-complete problems, so PARTITION is NP-complete.

Why it matters

PARTITION is the shortest decision form of “split numbers into two equal-sum groups”. It places NP-completeness, certificate verification, reduction direction and pseudo-polynomial DP on one chain, showing that “the problem is hard” is not “every instance is hard”.

It is a combinatorial decision problem, not a cryptographic algorithm; it is adjacent to subset sum, knapsack and exact cover, but the objective and reduction chain differ. Cryptographic hardness also needs average-case distributions and a concrete reduction, which NP-completeness alone does not supply.

Applications

  1. Establish difficulty: reduce a new problem to PARTITION, or PARTITION to the new problem; never reverse the direction. 2. Verify a certificate: a subset T summing to B is a polynomial-time NP certificate. 3. Exact small cases: use O(rB) dynamic programming when B is small; for large B use branch-and-bound or heuristics and state the lack of a worst-case bound. 4. State the encoding: numbers are binary encoded; the pseudo-polynomial bound is in the numeric value B, not its bit length.

Do not claim every instance is hard because PARTITION is NP-complete; do not present the pseudo-polynomial DP as polynomial; do not turn worst-case NP-completeness into a cryptographic assumption.

Limits & attacks

  1. Special cases: equal numbers, small B or many duplicates are easy; NP-completeness only asserts the existence of hard instance families. 2. Pseudo-polynomial: the O(rB) DP can be 2^n in the binary length n of B; it is not polynomial time. 3. Reduction direction: reducing PARTITION to KNAPSACK only places PARTITION within KNAPSACK's difficulty; it does not prove KNAPSACK hard. 4. Cryptographic boundary: NP-completeness gives no average-case hardness, random self-reduction or concrete security parameter; this entry is not a cryptographic protocol proof.

History

Karp's 1972 “Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems” listed 21 combinatorial problems as NP-complete, including PARTITION; the paper gives an explicit KNAPSACK α PARTITION construction and starts the reduction chain from SATISFIABILITY.
