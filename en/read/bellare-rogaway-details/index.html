Bellare–Rogaway Authenticated Key Exchange

Article review and catalog-metadata acceptance are recorded separately. The library remains under construction.

Principles

Theorem: Bellare–Rogaway Authenticated Key Exchange (Bellare–Rogaway 认证密钥交换). ### Parties, oracles and sessions

The model writes the s-th session of party i with peer j as oracle Π^s_{i,j}. Adversary E may issue send, reveal and test queries. A conversation is a time-ordered message sequence; two oracles have a matching conversation when their message prefixes correspond one-for-one in time order. ### Authentication and key indistinguishability

Protocol Π is a secure AKE when both conditions hold: under a benign adversary, matching oracles both accept and α^s_{i,j}=α^t_{j,i} is sampled from S_k; and for every PPT adversary E the advantage

Adv^E(k)=max{0, Pr[GoodGuess^E(k)]−1/2}

The adversary selects a fresh oracle: accepted, not revealed, and with no revealed matching partner. The challenger flips b: b=0 returns the real α and b=1 a random sample from S_k; E outputs Guess. GoodGuess is the event Guess=b. ### The AKEP1 construction

A shared 2k-bit long-term key is split into a1 and a2; a1 drives PRF f for message authentication and a2 drives f′ to encrypt the session key:

{α}{a2}=(r, f′{a2}(r)⊕α). B samples α from S_k and places this ciphertext in Text2; the MAP2 flow authenticates with f_{a1}. Theorem 5.2 states that AKEP1 is a secure AKE when f and f′ are PRFs; in the random MAP1 experiment, acceptance without matching is at most T_E(k)^2·2^−k.

Why it matters

Bellare–Rogaway separate “both parties accept” from “the adversary cannot distinguish the session key”. Message authentication alone is insufficient because both parties may accept while the adversary still distinguishes the key; key indistinguishability alone is insufficient because without a matching conversation the key cannot be attributed to the right peer.

Applications

  1. Define sessions: give every party, peer and session a unique oracle so sessions cannot be confused. 2. Prove authentication: first prove correctness under a benign adversary, then bound acceptance without a matching conversation. 3. Prove key security: reduce the test query to a PRF or ideal-cipher assumption and state freshness precisely. 4. Separate keys: use distinct PRF domains for authentication and encryption, such as a1 and a2.

Do not treat “both parties accept” as AKE security; do not reveal the target session after the test query; do not present this model as a proof of forward secrecy or UC security.

Limits & attacks

  1. Model boundary: long-term key compromise, temporary-key compromise, forward secrecy and key escrow are outside this definition; a stronger model is needed. 2. Session binding: matching depends on unique session identifiers and message order; if a protocol permits reordering, reflection or session confusion, the definition cannot add identity binding by itself. 3. Implementation: replacing the PRF with a hash lacking domain separation, reusing randomness or mismanaging state bypasses the reduction assumptions. 4. Overclaiming: the two-party AKEP1 of this paper is not every AKE protocol; this entry is not implementation certification and does not claim to cover every unknown-key-share variant.

History

The paper appeared at CRYPTO '93, proceedings pages 232–249. It placed entity authentication and session-key distribution in one security game, defined matching conversations, freshness and test advantage, and constructed AKEP1 from PRFs.
