MACsec link encryption (IEEE 802.1AE)

Article review and catalog-metadata acceptance are recorded separately. The library remains under construction.

Principles

Standard: MACsec link encryption (IEEE 802.1AE) (MACsec 链路加密（IEEE 802.1AE）). ### A security header inside the Ethernet frame

MACsec inserts a SecTAG after the source MAC address and an ICV at the frame end, optionally encrypting the frame body. SecTAG is 8 or 16 bytes: 2-byte EtherType 0x88E5, 1-byte TCI/AN, 1-byte SL, 4-byte PN and an optional 8-byte SCI. The SCI combines a 48-bit MAC with a 16-bit port number and identifies a unidirectional secure channel. ### Cipher suites

The current standard centres on AES-GCM: GCM-AES-128 is mandatory; GCM-AES-256 and GCM-AES-XPN-128/256 are optional. GCM provides confidentiality and integrity together; the IV is built from the SCI (or SSCI under XPN) and the PN. The default PN is 32 bits; extended packet numbering uses 64 bits so a single SAK does not hit the 2^32-frame limit too early.

Why it matters

MACsec protects link-layer frames, so ARP, LLDP, IP and MPLS all ride under the same protection regardless of whether higher layers enable TLS or IPsec. It suits switch-to-switch, router-to-router, data-centre leaf-spine links and carrier Ethernet that needs line-rate encryption.

MACsec defines frame protection only; key agreement lives in MACsec Key Agreement (MKA) in IEEE 802.1X-2010. Devices authenticate with a long-term CAK, elect a key server, and the server generates and distributes short-term SAKs; SAK rotation does not break the link, and the two-bit AN lets one secure channel hold four simultaneous security associations.

Applications

MACsec can protect a point-to-point link or an end-to-end path with intermediate devices; it is common in campus cores, data-centre interconnect, carrier Ethernet and automotive Ethernet. Hardware MACsec engines encrypt and decrypt at line rate, suitable for 10G/25G/100G ports.

MACsec does not replace TLS, IPsec or application encryption. It protects one hop or one segment, and the switches at both ends see plaintext; true end-to-end confidentiality still comes from an upper-layer protocol. Deployment must also cover key management, device authentication, replay windows and failover.

Limits & attacks

The receiver verifies the ICV and drops a frame on failure; the PN increases, and the replay window tolerates some reordering while rejecting too-old frames. The SL field handles short frames, and the confidentiality offset can encrypt only part of the frame body (offset 0/30/50 bytes) when some protocol headers must remain visible.

MACsec stops at the link layer; it cannot stop leakage inside the devices at either end, nor replace end-point encryption. It depends on correct MKA configuration, CAK protection and SAK rotation; wrong keys, disabled validation or an oversized replay window all weaken security. The ICV can be 8 or 16 bytes, with 16 bytes (128 bits) common in deployment.

History

IEEE 802.1AE-2006 first standardized MACsec; 802.1AE-2018 is the main current edition. 802.1AEbw-2013 added 64-bit extended packet numbering (XPN), and 802.1X-2010 defined MKA. Later revisions brought in GCM-AES-256 and related suites.

MACsec moved from carrier equipment into data-centre switches and automotive Ethernet; it layers with IPsec and TLS: MACsec protects a hop, IPsec protects the IP layer, TLS protects an application session. They are complementary, not mutually exclusive, and are stacked according to the trust boundary.
