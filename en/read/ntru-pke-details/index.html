NTRU Lattice Public-Key Encryption

Article review and catalog-metadata acceptance are recorded separately. The library remains under construction.

Principles

Algorithm: NTRU (NTRU 公钥加密). ### Ring and keys

NTRU uses short private polynomials f,g in the polynomial ring modulo x^N−1 and public key h=p·g/f mod q; encryption is c=r·h+m, and decryption computes f·c, center-lifts and reduces modulo p to recover m. Here q is a power of two and p=3. ### Encryption and decryption

Encode the message as a short polynomial m and pick a short random r; the ciphertext is c=r·h+m mod q. Decryption computes a=f·c mod q, center-lifts to [−q/2,q/2), then computes m=f_p^{-1}·a mod p. Correctness requires the noise f·r·g to stay in range. ### KEM and parameters

The NIST submission turns NTRU into an IND-CCA2 KEM: encapsulation derives m and r from a random seed and hashes the ciphertext; decapsulation failure outputs an implicit rejection. ntruhps2048509 uses N=509 and q=2048, with a 699-byte public key/ciphertext, a 935-byte private key and a 32-byte shared secret.

Why it matters

NTRU encryption is polynomial multiplication plus one inversion, and its keys are hundreds of times smaller than McEliece’s; on a CPU it is faster than RSA/ECC at comparable security and resists quantum attacks.

It has more than two decades of public analysis, a simple structure and adjustable parameters, making it a conservative but usable lattice KEM: a roughly 0.7 KB public key suits TLS handshakes and embedded long-term keys. It has no worst-case reduction; security rests on the NTRU lattice assumption.

Applications

NTRU was a NIST PQC finalist (July 2020), but NIST selected ML-KEM in 2022 and NTRU did not enter a FIPS standard; ISO/IEC 29192-4 Amd 2 is standardizing the NTRU KEM and reached FDIS in October 2026.

It is used in Open Quantum Safe/liboqs, hybrid TLS, embedded firmware and long-term identity keys; ANSI X9.98 and IEEE P1363 had NTRUEncrypt profiles, and NTRU Prime is a separate branch.

Limits & attacks

Published parameters are not broken by lattice reduction (BKZ), but the decryption-failure rate must be provably negligible; failing ciphertexts can enable distinguishing or key recovery, so implementations must be constant-time and validate ciphertexts.

Center lifting, modular inversion and key generation carry side-channel/fault risk; weak or non-invertible f causes decryption failure; NTRU lacks a worst-case reduction, and while Shor does not apply directly, lattice algorithms need continued evaluation.

Boundaries: public keys/ciphertexts are larger than ML-KEM’s; encapsulation failure must be implicitly rejected; parameter sets cannot be mixed; NTRU and NTRU Prime use different rings and parameters.

History

Hoffstein, Pipher and Silverman introduced NTRU at ANTS III in 1998; NTRU Cryptosystems commercialized it; it was submitted to NIST PQC in 2018, became a finalist in 2020 and was not selected for a NIST standard in 2022; in 2025–2026 it moved to ISO/IEC 29192-4 Amd 2.

It is one of the earliest practical lattice public-key schemes and influenced NTRU Prime, NTRU+ and later designs.
