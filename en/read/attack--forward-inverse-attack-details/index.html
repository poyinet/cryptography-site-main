Forward–Inverse Meet-in-the-Middle Attack

Article review and catalog-metadata acceptance are recorded separately. The library remains under construction.

Principles

Attack: Forward–Inverse Meet-in-the-Middle Attack (正反向中间相遇攻击). ### Composition and the middle state

Let a block cipher E have key space {0,1}^k. Two-key double encryption is C=E_{K2}(E_{K1}(P)). The forward–inverse attack does not enumerate 2^{2k} key pairs: it enumerates 2^k single keys from each side and treats the middle state as the meeting point. ### Forward table and backward scan

Fix one known plaintext–ciphertext pair (P,C). Compute X=E_{K1}(P) forward and store (X,K1) in table T; compute Y=D_{K2}(C) backward and look up Y in T. If X=Y, then (K1,K2) is a candidate pair; a second pair verifies it and filters false alarms. ### Complexity

The table has M=2^k entries, and each side performs about 2^k block operations, so total time is T≈2^{k+1}. For double DES (k=56), this is about 2^57 operations and 2^56 intermediate states. For block size n, false alarms are about 2^{2k−n}; a second pair makes them negligible.

Why it matters

Double encryption alone expands the key space from 2^k to 2^{2k}, but meet-in-the-middle splits the search into two 2^k tables. It shows that adding key lengths is not the same as adding security, and it is a key counterexample for multiple encryption, key separation and block-size choices.

This is a generic time–memory trade-off, not a break of one block cipher. Unlike differential or linear cryptanalysis, it uses composition structure rather than round-function bias; it therefore applies to any composable cipher, at a high memory cost.

Applications

  1. Check the conditions: one known plaintext–ciphertext pair; encryption decomposes into two independent-key stages; the middle space is small enough to store. 2. Build the table: enumerate K1 forward and store X with K1. 3. Scan: enumerate K2 backward and look up Y. 4. Filter: verify candidates with a second pair. 5. Report: time 2^{k+1}, memory 2^k, false-alarm rate 2^{2k−n}.

Do not quote time 2^{k+1} without the 2^k storage and access cost; do not apply the double-DES result directly to triple DES, AES, or compositions with key binding.

Limits & attacks

  1. Memory: 2^56 states remain enormous on real equipment; time–memory trade-offs (distinguished points, rainbow tables) change the parameters. 2. False alarms and uniqueness: one pair produces many candidates; a second pair or redundancy filters them. 3. Key independence: if K1 and K2 are derived from one master key or have algebraic relations, complexity can drop further; three independent keys are not directly covered. 4. Block size: the false-alarm rate 2^{2k−n} varies with n, and short blocks are worse.

History

Diffie and Hellman proposed the time–memory trade-off in their 1977 assessment of DES key search. Merkle and Hellman made the meet-in-the-middle attack explicit for double encryption in 1981, showing that a 112-bit two-key double encryption can be broken with about 2^56 operations and 2^56 words.

This result drove the deployment of triple DES and the later migration to AES. This entry covers the classic two-key meet-in-the-middle only; multidimensional meet-in-the-middle, partial matching and quantum search have different costs and must not be conflated.
