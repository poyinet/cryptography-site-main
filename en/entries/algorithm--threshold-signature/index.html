Threshold Signature

Principles

Algorithm: Threshold Signature (门限签名). ### Shamir sharing and Lagrange

Threshold signing Shamir-shares the group secret s into n shares, and any t shares reconstruct it via Lagrange coefficients; FROST uses two rounds—publish nonce commitments, then aggregate z=Σz_i into an ordinary Schnorr signature. ### Two-round FROST

In round one each signer generates a one-time nonce (d,e) and publishes commitments (D,E); in round two it computes z_i from the message, the commitment set and its share; the coordinator aggregates z=Σz_i. The result verifies like a single-signer Schnorr signature. ### DKG

Key generation can use a trusted dealer or distributed key generation; DKG lets parties obtain shares without ever holding the full s, at the cost of a more complex protocol.

Why it matters

Threshold signatures turn a single private key into t-of-n: fewer than t shares do not reveal s, and at least t honest parties must be online to sign. They suit exchange cold wallets, CA root keys, enterprise signing and blockchain validators.

FROST needs only two rounds and does not restrict concurrent signing sessions; its signatures are indistinguishable from ordinary Schnorr, so the threshold structure is invisible on-chain or in protocols.

Applications

It is used for blockchain custody and validators, CA and code signing, enterprise multisignature, DNSSEC and cross-institution approvals; RFC 9591 defines FROST and Ed25519/Ed448-compatible suites, with trusted-dealer or DKG key generation.

Requirements: never reuse a nonce; bind every signing session to a unique message and commitment set; the coordinator must verify partial signatures; DKG must handle malicious shares and complaints; a threshold signature is not multisig—only one public key appears on-chain.

Limits & attacks

The most damaging implementation error is nonce reuse: two signatures sharing the same (d,e) directly solve for a share and then the group private key; RFC 9591 requires one-time nonces and recommends erasing them after signing.

Boundaries: threshold signing protects the private key, not endpoints; a malicious coordinator can deny service or choose messages but cannot forge; t or more colluding parties can recover s; FROST is not post-quantum and relies on discrete logarithms.

History

FROST was proposed by Komlo and Goldberg in 2020 (ePrint 2020/852), entered CFRG in 2021 and became RFC 9591 in June 2024. It builds on Schnorr threshold signatures and Shamir sharing, improving earlier three-round and concurrency-limited schemes.

RFC 9591 is Informational, not an IETF standard; production deployments must apply domain separation for their curve and protocol.
