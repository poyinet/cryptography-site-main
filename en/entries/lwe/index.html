Learning With Errors

Principles

Algorithm: Learning With Errors (带误差学习问题). LWE fixes a public matrix A and modulus q; a secret vector s and a short error vector e satisfy b = A·s + e mod q. Without the error, Gaussian elimination recovers s. With it, the problem becomes finding a close vector in a high-dimensional lattice, which is believed hard even for quantum algorithms. Regev's contribution is the reduction linking worst-case lattice hardness to the indistinguishability of random LWE samples.

Why it matters

It turns lattice cryptography from a collection of constructions into a single analysable assumption. Kyber/ML-KEM, NewHope and FHE schemes trace back to LWE or its ring variant. For this site, LWE is the node that makes post-quantum KEMs, homomorphic encryption and the “quantum-resistant” story intelligible.

Restate the chain as input → state → transform → output → criterion. Every step should identify who acts, what changes and what evidence remains. A step without evidence is marked unverified, not reproduced.

Applications

Public-key encryption and key encapsulation, fully homomorphic encryption, identity-based encryption and pseudorandom functions. Deployments rarely use bare LWE; they use Ring-LWE or Module-LWE to shrink keys and multiplication cost.

Limits & attacks

LWE itself is not broken by Shor's algorithm. Risk comes from parameter choice, noise growth, decryption failure and implementation side channels. Too narrow an error distribution, too small a modulus or a biased sampler can turn the theoretical hardness into an attackable problem. It also provides no integrity or authentication and must be composed with AEAD and signatures.

History

Regev gave the worst-case reduction in 2005. Lyubashevsky, Peikert and Regev introduced Ring-LWE in 2010, moving arithmetic into polynomial rings. NIST's post-quantum process made Module-LWE the core of ML-KEM. It did not replace RSA overnight; parameters, reductions and implementations took more than a decade to mature.
