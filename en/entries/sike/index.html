Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation

The text and catalog metadata passed current review.

Principles

Algorithm: Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation (SIKE 超椭圆同源 KEM). SIKE wrapped the SIDH key exchange into a KEM. Two parties walk secret isogeny paths on supersingular elliptic curves; the public key contains torsion points on the image curve. When the paths meet, both obtain the same j-invariant as the shared secret. Its selling point was very small public keys and ciphertexts for bandwidth-constrained settings.

Why it matters

SIKE was an alternate candidate in NIST's post-quantum process and represented an isogeny assumption independent of lattices and codes. Its fall is instructive: the attack came from mathematics and auxiliary-point leakage, not from a quantum computer. “Post-quantum” does not mean “already secure”.

Applications

It should not be used as a production KEM. Its value now lies in cryptanalysis history, isogeny research and teaching how a candidate is eliminated.

Limits & attacks

In 2022 Castryck and Decru published an efficient key-recovery attack. Auxiliary torsion points exposed in the public key let an attacker turn reconstruction of the secret isogeny path into a computable isogeny problem. SIKE parameters fell quickly and NIST removed it from standardisation. The attack runs on classical computers.

History

Jao and De Feo proposed SIDH in 2011; SIKE entered NIST's second round in 2017. The attack appeared in July 2022 and was independently verified, eliminating SIKE. The lesson: post-quantum candidates need years of cryptanalysis, not early deployment because the keys look small and the paper looks elegant.
