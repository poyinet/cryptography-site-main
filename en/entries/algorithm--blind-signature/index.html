Blind Signature

Principles

Algorithm: Blind Signature (盲签名). ### Blind—sign—unblind

A blind signature lets a signer sign without seeing the message; the user later unblinds the signature into a valid signature on the original message. In RSA: the user picks random r, computes m’ = r^e·H(m) mod n and sends m’ to the signer; the signer returns s’=(m’)^d mod n; the user computes s=s’·r^−1 mod n, obtaining H(m)^d, a valid RSA signature on m. ### Three properties

Blindness: the signer cannot recover the message from the signing interaction. Unlinkability: the signer cannot match a signing session to a later signature/message. Unforgeability: the user cannot obtain more valid signatures than authorized, i.e. one-more-forgery security. ### Variants

RSA-FDH/RSABSSA uses PSS encoding; RFC 9474 defines an interoperable RSA blind signature. Schnorr/BLS blind signatures are shorter, but concurrent sessions must resist ROS attacks.

Why it matters

Blind signatures resolve the tension between “the signer must not know the content” and “verifiers must be able to verify the signature”. They underlie electronic cash, anonymous credentials, Privacy Pass, anonymous voting and blind tokens. The issuer signs only a blinded token, and later verification cannot link it to the issuance session.

They separate authentication from anonymity: the signature proves authorization, while blinding cuts the link between signer and user.

Applications

Typical uses include electronic cash/central-bank digital currency privacy, Privacy Pass-style anonymous tokens, anonymous credentials, electronic voting and license/timestamp tokens. RFC 9474 RSABSSA verifies with standard RSA-PSS, which fits existing PKIs.

Requirements: high-entropy, one-time blinding factors; RSA blinding and constant-time private-key operations; limits on concurrent sessions and signature counts; domain separation and structural checks so a blind-signature oracle cannot be abused across protocols.

Limits & attacks

Blind signatures are not absolute anonymity: predictable blinding, a colluding signer and verifier, or a low-entropy message space enables linking attacks. RSA blind signatures also rely on the RSA assumption and FDH/PSS encoding; a signer willing to sign any blinded message becomes a signing oracle.

Boundaries: a blind signature does not provide untraceable payments by itself and does not replace mixing or network anonymity; it only cuts the signer-to-message link. Concurrent Schnorr blind signatures must resist ROS attacks; RSA blind signatures need RSA blinding and constant-time private-key operations.

History

David Chaum introduced blind signatures in the 1982 CRYPTO paper “Blind Signatures for Untraceable Payments”, initially for untraceable electronic payments; the proceedings appeared in 1983, pp. 199–203. Chaum later used them in eCash. Bellare–Namprempre–Pointcheval–Semanko analyzed one-more RSA inversion in 2001, and RFC 9474 standardized RSABSSA in 2023.

The primitive remains foundational for privacy protocols, though deployments moved from eCash to Privacy Pass, anonymous credentials and token services.
