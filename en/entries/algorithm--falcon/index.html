Principles

Algorithm: Falcon (Falcon 格签名). ### GPV hash-and-sign

Falcon is a GPV-style NTRU lattice signature: it hashes the message to a target point, uses a trapdoor short basis to sample a short lattice vector near that target, and outputs the vector; verification checks that it is short and lies in the correct coset. ### NTRU lattice and fast Fourier sampling

The private key is a solution (f,g,F,G) of the NTRU equation fG−gF=q, forming a short basis of an NTRU lattice. Signing recursively splits the problem in the FFT domain and samples at the leaves with the discrete Gaussian SamplerZ in O(n log n). Security reduces to SIS over NTRU lattices. ### Parameters

Falcon-512 has an 897-byte public key and a signature averaging about 666 bytes; Falcon-1024 has a 1793-byte public key and a signature averaging about 1280 bytes. Signatures are variable-length, so verification must enforce the parameter set’s upper bound.

Why it matters

Falcon targets the smallest public-key-plus-signature footprint: among the NIST lattice signatures it has the smallest combined size and the fastest verification, which suits bandwidth- and storage-constrained TLS certificates, JOSE/COSE tokens and embedded firmware signatures.

It needs no AES or large matrices; verification is polynomial multiplication plus hashing. Fast Fourier sampling lets it reach thousands of signatures per second on a general CPU, with verification five to ten times faster.

Applications

It is used in TLS 1.3 FN-DSA drafts, JOSE/COSE FN-DSA-512/1024, code signing and firmware signing; NIST is standardizing it as FIPS 206.

Requirements: fresh random salt per signature; constant-time Gaussian sampling; guard floating-point FFT against NaN, exceptional values and side channels across platforms; never reuse internal randomness across signatures.

Limits & attacks

Falcon reduces to NTRU-SIS, but implementation risk dominates: signing uses double-precision floating point and discrete Gaussian sampling, so cache, timing and fault attacks can recover the private key; missing randomness enables differential fault attacks.

Boundaries: signatures are variable-length, so protocols must budget the upper bound; Falcon is unsuitable when deterministic signatures are required; only 512/1024 parameter sets exist and cannot be rescaled; verification must check encoding length, ranges and subgroups.

History

Falcon was designed by Fouque, Hoffstein, Kirchner, Lyubashevsky, Pornin, Prest, Ricosset, Seiler, Whyte and Zhang and submitted to NIST PQC on 30 November 2017. NIST selected it in 2022; the standardized name is FN-DSA and the standard is FIPS 206, expected in late 2026 or early 2027.

Its predecessor is NTRUSign; fast Fourier sampling comes from the Ducas–Prest 2016 trapdoor-sampling work.
