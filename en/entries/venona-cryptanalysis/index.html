Principles

Attack: VENONA Cryptanalysis (VENONA 破译). ### Codebook plus one-time pad

Soviet trade, diplomatic and intelligence messages were looked up in a codebook to obtain x_i, then superenciphered with a one-time-pad stream k_i by digitwise addition modulo 10:

c_i=(x_i+k_i) mod 10. If k_i is truly random, used once and as long as x_i, the ciphertext is perfectly secret about the message; VENONA’s opening was not the algorithm but reuse of pad pages. ### Reuse and depth

When two messages reuse the same page:

c_i⊖c_i′=(x_i⊖x_i′) mod 10. The mask cancels, leaving differences between two codebook groups. More duplicate pages and longer messages give more depth; codebook frequency, known fragments and English/Russian cribs then recover group meanings and translations.

Why it matters

VENONA shows that the security condition of a one-time pad is non-reuse. It turns “key reuse” from an abstract prohibition into an operational case: once reused, the OTP degenerates into a statistically analyzable running-key problem.

JN-25’s additive book was reused by design; VENONA’s OTP should have been used once but about 35,000 duplicate pages created depth. Their institutional settings and failure modes differ, and both should not be described as “additive-book reuse”.

Applications

  1. Find duplicate pages by matching ciphertext sequences at the same positions. 2. Align messages and subtract digitwise to obtain codebook differences. 3. Recover groups with depth, frequency and cribs. 4. Look up the codebook to obtain plaintext; use language guesses for unresolved fragments. 5. Record coverage and translation confidence.

Do not present a partial decrypt as the full text; do not ignore codebook changes and copying errors; do not claim the OTP algorithm itself was broken.

Limits & attacks

  1. Correct OTP use is unbreakable: truly random, non-reused, message-length keys defeat VENONA methods. 2. Duplicate pages are rare: about 35,000 duplicated pages are still a minority of huge traffic, and only part of the messages can be read. 3. Codebook changes or compilation errors produce systematic misreads. 4. Translation bottleneck: even recovered groups can be misread in names, abbreviations and Russian grammar; a partial decrypt is not the full text.

History

In February 1943 the U.S. Army Signal Intelligence Service began the secret program later codenamed VENONA; depth clues appeared in 1945, and Meredith Gardner read the first message in February 1946; the project ran until October 1980. From July 1995, six releases made about 3,000 translations public.

Public records confirm about 35,000 duplicate key pages, but not every duplicate page was exploitable. This entry covers the mechanism, scale and public timeline; it does not turn intelligence-history conclusions into cryptographic theorems.
